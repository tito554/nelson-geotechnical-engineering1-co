In Nelson, the ground conditions shift dramatically within a few hundred metres—from the dense gravels of the Waimea Plains to the softer estuarine silts around the Haven. What we encounter most often in this region are sites where isolated footings simply cannot distribute load safely, either because of variable fill or the presence of liquefiable layers at shallow depth. A raft or mat foundation becomes the only rational solution when you need to bridge these inconsistencies. The design process here draws heavily on NZGS guidelines and the loading combinations of NZS 4203, but the real skill lies in interpreting the borehole data against the local geology—particularly the Moutere Gravel and the overlying Quaternary deposits that define so much of Nelson’s building land. When the site investigation reveals soft zones deeper than two metres, we often recommend a stiffened raft with perimeter thickening rather than a uniform slab, and we coordinate closely with the driller to ensure the SPT drilling data captures the transition between the alluvium and the competent gravels below.

A properly designed raft foundation in Nelson transforms a site with marginal bearing capacity into a unified structural element that resists settlement, liquefaction, and lateral spread.

Service characteristics in Nelson

The engineering behaviour of Nelson’s soils varies notably between the flatlands around Stoke and Richmond and the sloping sections that climb toward the Grampians. In Stoke, the profile typically shows a layer of silty sand overlying the Moutere Gravel—a material that provides excellent bearing once you reach it, but which often sits beneath compressible or liquefaction-prone horizons that demand a mat foundation to reduce differential settlement. Hillside sites in areas like Atawhai present a different challenge altogether, where cut-and-fill benching creates a transition zone of variable stiffness across the building platform. A well-designed raft in these conditions incorporates a grid of stiffening beams integrated with a suspended ground slab, effectively creating a rigid plate that resists distortion even when one edge bears on natural ground and the other on compacted fill. The thickness of the raft element is governed by span-to-depth ratios for the beam spacing, but in Nelson we rarely drop below 250 mm for the slab component, and edge beams often go to 600 mm or more where expansive clay is present in the weathered zone. The 2016 Kaikōura earthquake provided a stark reminder that even here, 200 km from the rupture, site-specific seismic design for mat foundations is not optional—it is essential.

Critical ground factors in Nelson

The rig we mobilise for the ground investigation in Nelson is a compact rubber-tracked drill capable of accessing rear sections with limited clearance, which is a common constraint on the city’s narrow hillside lots. It allows us to push SPTs and retrieve undisturbed samples without excessive disturbance to the site, and in many cases we can set up within a single car park space. The primary risk that a raft foundation addresses in this region is differential settlement caused by lateral variability in the soil profile—a condition we see repeatedly where old stream channels have been infilled and built over. Without a properly integrated mat, even a well-constructed timber-framed dwelling can develop serviceability issues within the first five years: plasterboard cracking, binding doors, and uneven floor levels. We also assess the potential for liquefaction-induced bearing failure, following the simplified procedure of Boulanger and Idriss, and cross-check against the MBIE guidance for foundation design in seismic areas. The raft’s rigidity is modelled explicitly in our finite element analysis, ensuring that the post-liquefaction residual strength of the soil is not exceeded under the design earthquake.

Our services

The raft foundation design is always part of a broader geotechnical and structural package tailored to the specific demands of a Nelson site. These are the two core service components we deliver alongside the foundation design itself.

Integrated Geotechnical Investigation and Raft Design

We combine targeted drilling, laboratory testing of recovered samples, and advanced numerical modelling to produce a raft foundation design that is calibrated to the actual ground conditions at your Nelson site. The investigation follows NZGS guidelines for logging and sampling, and the design output includes reinforcement layouts, beam schedules, and a construction specification that a local contractor can execute without ambiguity.

Seismic Liquefaction Assessment for Mat Foundations

For sites within the Nelson-Tasman liquefaction susceptibility zones, we carry out a detailed assessment of the post-liquefaction bearing capacity and settlement. This directly informs the raft geometry and the required depth of ground improvement, if any, and is documented in a Producer Statement (PS1) ready for council consent.

FAQ

What is the typical cost range for a raft foundation design for a residential dwelling in Nelson?

For a standard single-dwelling site in the Nelson region, the professional engineering fee for a raft/mat foundation design—including the geotechnical investigation, structural analysis, and PS1 documentation—typically falls between NZ$2,000 and NZ$6,000. The final figure depends on site access, the extent of drilling required, and the complexity of the ground profile.

How does a raft foundation perform on Nelson’s liquefaction-prone soils compared to deep piles?

A well-designed raft distributes the building load over a large area, which reduces the demand on the soil and helps the structure move as a rigid body during a seismic event. In moderate liquefaction scenarios, this can be more cost-effective than deep piling, provided the post-liquefaction settlement is within acceptable limits. We model both options and often find that a raft with perimeter thickening achieves the required performance without the need for a full piled solution.

How long does the design and consent process take in Nelson City Council?

The design itself typically takes three to four weeks from the completion of the site investigation. Council processing times vary, but a well-prepared consent package with a clear Producer Statement and supporting geotechnical report generally moves through the engineering review without significant delays. We manage the RFI process directly with council engineers to keep the project on programme.
